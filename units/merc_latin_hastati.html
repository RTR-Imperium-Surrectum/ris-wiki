Mercenary Latin Hastati

← all units · wiki index

Mercenary. Hired from a regional pool, not recruited from a building.

Class: spearmen · Category: infantry · Men per unit: 40

Description

"The Latins, Campanians, Sabines, Umbrians, Volscians, Marrucini, Peligni, Ferentani, and their other subjects they divided into four divisions and mingled them with the Roman legions, in order that no part of their lines might be weak." — Dionysius of Halicarnassus (Roman Antiquities 20.1.5)

Similar to their Roman kindred, these Hastati form the first battle line of the Latin armies. They are the youngest of the heavy infantry and lead the opening clash of arms, thus suffering the most casualties in the army. Levied from the cities and colonies of Latium, these young warriors take the brunt of an enemy attack, using their vigorous and energetic spirit to disrupt and rout enemy forces. In battle, they are armed with dual-purpose hastae (thus their name) that can be used as heavy javelins as well as for close-quarters fighting. For defence, each soldier carries a large oval scutum with a central vertical spine and wooden or bronze boss. Protected by bronze Montefortino or other types of Italic helmets and, for some, bronze pectoral armour, they advance in an open manipular formation, absorbing missile volleys, exhausting the enemy, and creating openings that might be exploited by the more experienced men behind them.

Historical background

The Latin communities of Central Italy shared a common linguistic, religious, and cultural heritage with Rome, traditionally organised through the Latin League centred on the sanctuary at the spring of Ferentina beneath the Alban Mount (Festus, Gloss. Lat. 276L; Drogula, 'The institutionalization of warfare in early Rome', in: Armstrong and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 26–27; Momigliano, s.v. 'The origins of Rome', in: The Cambridge Ancient History VII.2 (2006), pp. 84–85). Under older treaties such as the foedus Cassianum, Roman and Latin forces went to war as equal partners, administering command (imperium) through mutual council and rotating military leadership (Festus 276L; Drogula (2020), pp. 26–27; Cornell, s.v. 'The conquest of Italy', in: The Cambridge Ancient History VII.2 (2006), p. 351–356).

Later during the First Samnite War, this created tensions between the Romans and their Latin allies to the point that Annius of Setia, one of the leaders of the Latins, went to Rome and spoke the following before the Senate: “But since you cannot bring your minds to abandon your impudent claims to sovereignty, we will go so far, in recognising that we are kindred nations, as to offer peace upon the conditions of equal rights for both, since it has pleased the gods to grant equal strength to both; though we are quite able to assert the independence of Latium by force of arms. One consul must be elected from Rome, the other from Latium; the senate must contain an equal number of members from both nations; there must be one nation, one republic. And in order that there may be one seat of government and one name for all, since one side or the other must make some concession, let us, if this City really takes precedence, be all called Romans” (Liv. 8.5.3).

The Senate was outraged by this speech and refused their demands, thus starting the Latin War (340–338 BC). Ancient historians later remarked that, as the two opposing armies stood before each other, they were equal in arms and organization (Livy 8.6.15, 8.8.1–2). As Livy records: "The battle was very much like one fought in a civil war, so completely was there nothing among the Latins different from the Roman model except their spirit" (Liv. 8.8.2).

Following the defeat and dissolution of the Latin League in 338 BC, Rome initiated the “Latin Settlement’’ which brought the entirety of Latium under direct Roman control (Liv. 8.14.1–12; Tan, ‘The dilectus-tributum system’, in: Armstrong and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 59–61; Armstrong, ‘Organized Chaos’, in: ibid. (2020), pp. 76–77). Communities retaining Latin status, mainly the network of Latin colonies such as Cales, Fregellae, and Luceria, were organized as military allies (socii nominisve Latini) (Tan (2020), pp. 58–60; VanderPuy, ‘The price of expansion’, in: Armstrong and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 44–46). The terms of this “Settlement” included a massive expansion of both Roman citizenship, through the inclusion of various settlements as civitas and civitas sine suffragio, and the number of Roman allies. As a result, Rome’s available military manpower increased exponentially in this period, which proved necessary, as Rome’s appetite for manpower grew the more it continued to expand both northward and southward over the following decades (Armstrong (2020), p. 76).

Latin hastati were from then on levied and funded directly by their own magistrates to form the frontline maniples of the allied wings (alae sociorum), fighting in identical fashion beside the Roman legions throughout the Samnite and Pyrrhic Wars (Tan (2020), pp. 59–61; Helm, 'Poor man's war – rich man's fight, in: Armstrong and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 100–102). Tan points out that "the archaic set of rights and duties which came with Latin status (ius Latini) was, after 338, held almost exclusively by the Latin colonies". In addition, the fiscal interchangeability of Latin and non-Latin allies was expressed in the term socii nominisve Latini (“allies or those of the Latin name”) as part of the agrarian law of 111 BC, the difference being resumed to a few rights of interaction with the Romans (Tan (2020), p. 60). Thus integrated into the Roman military system as autonomous allies (socii) obligated to supply specified troop numbers for Roman armies under the formula togatorum (a list maintained by the Romans for the purpose of determining the annual military contributions of the Italian allies), the Latins continued to serve in major Roman campaigns, including those against Pyrrhos at Ausculum in 279 BC (Dion. Hal. Ant. Rom. 20.1.5; Manconi, 'The Umbri', in: Farney and Bradley (eds.), The Peoples of Ancient Italy (2017); Baronowski, 'The "Formula Togatorum"', in: Historia 33-2 [1984]). Dionysius tells us that before the battle, the Latins and other Italic allies were divided into four divisions and mixed with the Roman legions to avoid weakening any point of the line. This was done to ensure a uniform distribution of infantry strength, preventing Pyrrhos' phalanx or war elephants from exploiting a weak point or breaking a purely allied wing. However, it also shows that the Latins, along with the other Italic allies, were not as heavily armed as the Romans themselves, which might reflect the progressive subordination of Italian forces under Roman command.
