Mercenary Latin Principes

Main campaignRIS Classic

← all units · wiki index

Mercenary. Hired from a regional pool, not recruited from a building.

Class: heavy · Category: infantry · Men per unit: 40

Description

“Behind them were stationed an equal number of companies, called principes, made up of men in the full vigour of life, all carrying shields and furnished with superior weapons.” – Livy (History of Rome 8.8.6)

The Latin Principes are positioned in the second line (acies) of the legion and form the more experienced core of the Latin heavy infantry line. Composed of men in the prime of life, the principes enter combat equipped with magnificent arms, ready to deliver the crushing blow after the skirmishers and hastati have taken the brunt of the initial fighting. In combat, they are armed with heavy throwing pila, double-edged Celtic swords, and large oval scuta with reinforced spines. Unlike the younger hastati, all the principes are equipped with body armour, alongside a bronze greave fitted to the left leg, as well as Montefortino-style helmets topped with imposing feathers. Moving through the intervals left between the maniples of the hastati, they stand ready to advance into the frontline to relieve their hard-pressed Latin countrymen to decide the battle in a vicious close-quarters melee.

Historical background

The principes formed the second line of the Latin and Roman manipular order (15 manipuli in Livy 8.8.6; 10 speirai in Polyb. 6.21.7). Their title, derived from princeps ("the foremost/ the first/ the leaders"), confused ancient authors, who noted that the unit occupied the second rather than the first rank of the army (Varro, Ling. 5.89; Vegetius 2.15.3, 3.14.5; Dionysios of Halikarnassos, Ant. Rom. 20.11.2). Modern scholars suggest that the principes were the front-line fighters (like the Greek “promachoi” – “front fighters”) of the archaic hoplite phalanx formation prior to the adoption of the manipular checkerboard system, retaining their designation after becoming the second line (Jähns, Geschichte des Kriegswesens (1880), p. 215; Kromayer and Veith, Heerwesen und Kriegsführung (1923), p. 278).

As quoted at the start of this description from Livy’s narrative of the Latin War (340–338 BC), the principes of the Latins and Romans are singled out as possessing the most magnificent arms (‘insignibus maxime armis’: Liv. 8.8.6). These experienced soldiers carried the heavy oval scutum (Burns, The Cultural and Military Significance (2005), pp. 157–164; Armstrong, ‘Organized Chaos’, in: idem and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 84–86). For body defence, they wore bronze muscle cuirasses, anatomical pectoral bronze armour, or composite linen corselets (linothorakes), which were very common in Latium and Campania in the 4th to early 3rd century BC, paired with bronze Montefortino helmets decorated with triple-feather crests, as described by Polybios (VI.23.12) and a greave on the left leg (Liv. 9.40.3; Polyb. 6.23.12–15; Burns (2005), pp. 82–107, 108–114). In addition to the pilum (javelin), they were armed with double-edged slashing and thrusting swords of the Celtic type. A 4th-century BC specimen recovered at San Vittore in Lazio, for example, was inscribed: ‘Tr[ebonius] Pomponius, (son of) Gaius, made (m)e in Rome’ (Taylor, Panoply and Identity during the Roman Republic (2020), p. 36).

Under older treaties such as the foedus Cassianum, Roman and Latin forces went to war as equal partners, administering command (imperium) through mutual council and rotating military leadership between themselves. However, as Rome expanded and imposed its hegemony over Latium, tensions between the two kindred people rose and devolved into war (Festus 276L; Drogula, 'The institutionalization of warfare in early Rome', in: Armstrong and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 26–27; Cornell, s.v. 'The conquest of Italy', in: The Cambridge Ancient History VII.2 (2006), p. 351–356). The Battle of Vesuvius in 340 BC was the first recorded battle of the Latin War, in which the Romans, with their allies the Samnites, faced a coalition of several peoples: Latins, Campanians, Volsci, Sidicini, and Aurunci. Livy records that “the two armies were drawn up in the same formation, and they knew that if the maniples kept their order they would have to fight, not only vexilla with vexilla, hastati with hastati, principes with principes, but even centurion with centurion” (Liv. 8.8–10; Armstrong (2020), pp. 84–86). As the battle lines collided, the Roman hastati were driven back by the Latins, forcing the Roman principes to step forward through the intervals to meet the Latin principes, locking the two armies in a prolonged, bloody stalemate. The day was only won for the Romans once they committed their rear reserves, with the triarii charging and throwing the front ranks of the Latins into disorder. They advanced “with such a slaughter that they left hardly a fourth part of the enemy” (Liv. 8.9.12–14, 8.10.1–5).

Following the defeat and dissolution of the Latin League in 338 BC, Rome initiated the “Latin Settlement”, bringing Latium under direct Roman hegemony (Liv. 8.14.1–12; Tan, 'The dilectus-tributum system’, in: Armstrong and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 56–60; Armstrong (2020), pp. 76–77). Those Latin communities not directly incorporated and given Roman citizenship still kept their sovereignty and possessed the rights of conubium and commercium with Roman citizens, though they were forbidden from exercising those same rights or conducting political relations among themselves. This arrangement did not isolate these communities entirely, however, since the majority of the old Latin peoples, whose territory bordered on theirs, were now Roman citizens, whom they were allowed to engage with (Cornell (2006), p. 366).

From this point onwards, Latin status (nomen Latinum) ceased to be restricted solely by ethnic or religious identity, becoming instead a transferable legal status with defined rights and privileges that could be exercised in dealings with Roman citizens. Subsequently, it was conferred by Roman enactment upon newly founded Latin colonies, such as Cales (334 BC), Fregellae (328 BC), and Luceria (314 BC) (Cornell (2006), pp. 366–368; Tan (2020), pp. 58–60). Serving within allied cohorts (cohortes Latinae), Latin principes were levied and funded by their own municipal treasuries without drawing Roman stipendia. They provided Rome with heavily armoured, disciplined infantry that formed the backbone of the allied wings during the conquest of Italy and the Mediterranean. During the Gallic crisis of 225 BC (prior to the Battle of Telamon), Rome compiled a census of total allied military manpower available, and the combined manpower of the Latins alone was composed of “eighty thousand foot and five thousand horse", showing the massive amount of troops that the Latin allies could put to the field (Polyb. 2.24.10, 6.26.7; Tan (2020), pp. 59–61; Helm, 'Poor man's war – rich man's fight', in: Armstrong and Fronda (eds.), Romans at War (2020), pp. 104–106).
