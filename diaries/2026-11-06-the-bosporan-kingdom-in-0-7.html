The Bosporan Kingdom in 0.7

2026-11-06 · ahowl11

Disclaimer - This Dev diary was written by @Mausolos of Mylasa our lead historian, I am simply posting it. All screens are taken by the wonderful @Lollygagging

Welcome back to another dev diary, and with every single one we are getting closer to the release 🙂 This time, we jump over the Black Sea from Pontos to learn about one of the dominions under his control, the Bosporan Kingdom.

In the 6th century BC, Ionian Greeks, intrepid and ambitious, sailed to the northern shores of the Black Sea, where they founded settlements on either side of the Kimmerian Bosporos, the modern Strait of Kerch - chief among them Pantikapaion on the European and Phanagoreia on the Asian side (Strab. 7.4.4). In no time, these Greek settlements became rich export harbours of grain, cattle and slaves, and to defend themselves better, they established a loose federation led by a noble family called the Archainaktids in 480/479 BC. In 438/437 BC, Spartokos, commander of the Thracian mercenaries they had hired to secure the outside borders of the Bosporan cities, overthrew his lords and established a new dynasty (Diod. 12.31.1). They ruled a relatively small, autonomous and only formally united confederation of Greek cities which was perched between the Sindian and Maiotian kingdoms in the east (on the Kuban peninsula) and the Taurians and Crimean Scythians in the west (on, well, Crimea). This began to change when Leukon I ascended to the throne in 389 BC. In two bloody wars, he conquered the Sindian realm and took the title king of the Sindians for himself (Polya., Strat. 8.55; CIRB 1037; CIRB 1038). In the west, he attacked the rich Greek colonies of Theodosia and Chersonesos, but was thwarted by their protector, the Pontic Herakleia (Ps.-Arist. Oec. 2.2.8, Polyaenus, Strat. 6.9.3 & 4). When a civil war broke out in Herakleia during the 360s, however, Leukon seized the opportunity to take Theodosia (Dem. 20 (Lept.).33). Some of the neighbouring Scythians were also integrated into the realm and the army at this time (Polya. Strat. 6.9.4).

Scythian warriors on the electrum Kul-Oba vase, the inspiration for our Scytho-Bosporan units. Hermitage.

Leukon's son Pairisades I rounded off these conquests by subjugating the (remaining) Maiotians, the Thateans and Doschians in the interior of the Kuban peninsula (CIRB 971, 972, 1015). The Bosporan kingdom was thus a multicultural state much like the later Hellenistic kingdoms, with Scythians, Sindians, Maiotians, Greeks and Thracians living on its territory and also serving in its military. By the mid 3rd century, the rulers of the Kimmerian Bosporos had fully taken the title as king (basileus), even vis-a-vis their Greek subjects (Saprykin, The Archaeanactids of Bosporus( 2014), p. 297). The title is definitely attested for Pairisades II (r. ca. 284-245 BC), who reigns during the start of the RIS campaign and who possessed friendly and commercial ties to both Antigonids and Ptolemies (Rostovzeff, Collection of Greek Coins (1998), p. 232; Zenon PLond 1973/SP 1.90A).

In the latter phase of the 3rd century, the Bosporan kingdom suffered an economic crisis (Stolyarik, The Reign and Chronology of the Archon Hygiaenon (1998), p. 63), and the pressure of ever more Sarmatians in the area and the revived Royal Scythians increased the crisis in the 2nd century BC. In 110/109 BC, the latter attacked the Bosporan kingdom and killed Pairisades V. Diophantos, commander of Mithridates VI of Pontos, intervened, and added the Bosporan kingdom to his lord's realm (IPE I² 352; IOSPE³ III 8).

Reverse of a bronze obol of Pairisades II, ca. 270 BC. Bow with arrow, PAN for Pantikapaion

In 63 BC, Mithridates was betrayed by his son Pharnakes, who made himself king of the Kimmerian Bosporos and became its first ruler to be an amicus populi Romani ("friend of the Roman people"; App. Mith. 16.113). Pharnakes apparently thought little of the title, however, and betrayed the Romans at the first opportunity: When Caesar was in Alexandria during the civil war, Pharnakes overran Anatolia to reclaim Pontos and Cappadocia. Caesar hurried to Zela, however, defeated Pharnakes and concluded: Veni, vidi, vici (App. Mith. 120-121, Cass. Dio 42.45-46; Plut. Caesar 50). At home, the strategos Asandros had rebelled and taken the throne in his absence, and when Caesar captured Pharnakes and equipped his henchman Mithridates of Pergamon to take the Bosporos. Asandros was ready: At sea, he defeated Mithridates and his Roman reinforcements before they could touch land (Strab. 13.4.3.).

Asandros married Dynamis, the daughter of Pharnakes, to stabilise his rule (Cass. Dio 54.24.4), and in fact he would remain Bosporan monarch for almost three decades. Having re-established friendly relations with Rome (CIRB 30), he was ironically overthrown by a Roman renegade or former slave called Scribonius (Cass. Dio 54.24.4). Even though Scribonius, too, married Dynamis, he was quickly murdered by the Bosporan Greeks (Cass. Dio 54.24.5). Augustus then decided to reunite the Bosporos with Pontos under the pro-Roman king Polemon I, whose invasion ultimately ended in victory (Cass. Dio 54.24.5). In 9 BC, the Aspourgians, Sarmatians or Sindians in the east of the kingdom, rose and this king, too, found an early death (Strab. 11.2.11). Having married, in succession, Asandros, Scribonius and Polemon, the realm was now left to Dynamis alone who appeased both Romans and Aspourgians and ruled until her death in 7/8 AD.

Possible bust of queen Dynamis (r. ca. 47 BC-7/8 AD) from the Eremitage.

After Dynamis' death, Aspourgos - evidently a leader of the Aspourgians - founded a new dynasty. When he visited Rome in 13/14 AD, Tiberius bestowed the epithet philokaisar, friend of the emperor, on him (CIRB 39, CIRB 40 = IOSPE II, 36). Back home, he defeated Taurians and Crimean Scythians and thus brought most of Crimea under his control (CIRB 39, 40). His son Mithridates III succeeded him in 38 AD, but Kotys, the unhappy younger brother, told Claudius that Mithridates would betray Rome (Cass. Dio 60.28.7). Kotys was subsequently given Roman troops and ousted his brother from the throne. The war went on until 49 AD, when the Siraces, the Sarmatian allies of Mithridates, were finally overcome (Tac. ann. 12.17-18). While Mithradates was taken to Rome, Kotys I was a proper Roman client king, unlike many of his predecessors. Therefore, when he failed to support Olbia and Chersonesos against the Royal Scythians around 63 AD, Nero had him deposed and put the Kimmerian Bosporos under Roman military control (Ios. bell. Iud. 2.16.4 (366)).

Even in this situation, the Romans did not consider annexation - nor did they when Mithradates was captured and later executed in Rome for trying to assassinate Nero! (Plut. Galba 15). Instead, Kotys regained the throne after the suicide of the emperor in 68 AD and his son Rheskouporis I would inherit his position. His reign was stable and dominated by the re-establishment of a strong defense of the kingdom as well as amicable relations with Rome. This situation would hardly change over the next centuries, which are already outside of the RIS timeframe. The realm suffered heavily during the crisis of the 3rd century when Goths raided the Black Sea, but survived even the appearance of the Huns: The last known king of the Kimmerian Bosporos, Douptonos, lived around 500 AD (Vinogradov, The Late Classical Bosporus (1998/1999).

Ruins of a temple at Pantikapaion, capital of the Bosporan kingdom

The Bosporan Starting Position

After being conquered by Leukon, Sindians were integrated into the Bosporan army. They are depicted with thureos shields on coffins from the 3rd century BC (Mielczarek, Bosporan Army (1999), p. 54)

According to Herodotus, the Taurians, the natives of SE Crimea, were active as pirates on the Black Sea (Hdt. 4.103). Therefore, this unit combines Scythian, Greek and Anatolian equipment to make for a very decent heavy archer.

With the thureos becoming more widespread in the region in the 3rd century, heavily armoured infantry fighting with the machaira appear and increasingly replace hoplites (Mielczarek (1999), pp. 46-47).

The heavy swordsmen represent the Bosporan infantry under the Aspourgian dynasty in the 1st century AD. The Roman, Pontic and Sarmatian role models in regards to chainmail and big shields are apparent and can be found on many wall paintings and stelae from this time (Gajdukevic, Das Bosporanische Reich (1971), fig. 133-134; Tac. Ann. 12.15-16)

Scytho-Bosporan infantry is based on Scythian evidence like the Kul-Oba vase or the Solokha comb and represents the Scythian allies of the Bosporan Kingdom as well as the Crimean Scythians under the rule of the Spartokids.

Like elsewhere, thureophoroi also show up in the Kimmerian Bosporos in the 3rd century BC. On grave stelae, they usually have no armour and are thus a skirmisher/support unit (Mielczarek (1999), p. 55; pl. XIV-XV).

The hoplites represent the old upper middle class of the Greek cities in the realm and thus also fight with classical equipment as seen elsewhere in the Hellenic world.

The heavy spearmen also represent the finely armoured infantry of the 1st century AD, like the swordsmen, and are similarly armed aside from the spear.

Akontistai are well known from other faction as an emergency skirmisher unit for that has neither helmets nor armour.

Many Taurians fight as javelins with Scythian style shields and clothes since the epitomes of Apollodoros of Athens (which was probably composed by a later author in the 1st century AD) describes them as rather Scythian in appearance (Ps.-Apollod. Epit. 6.26)

These Greek slingers are no different from others, and like Red Zed always says, we have seen that many times before.

These traditional Greek peltasts are also the generic unit seen in other Greek rosters.

And of course the Greek Archers, which as the others, are widespread and have been shown numerous times.

A limestone statue from the Kerch Archaeology Museum, found in the Taman Peninsula in 1896, depicts a Sindian warrior with gorytos and bow, and therefore they, too, get their own archer unit.

The Taurian archers are, like the other Taurian units, inspired by Crimean Scythian, perhaps Taurian art like the Solokha Comb and by the local style of warfare.

The Maiotians were no nomads, but farmers, and yet archery was elementary for them, too, in both warfare and hunting (Strab. 11.2.4).

The Scytho-Bosporan archers are actually Scythian archers and thus experts in ranged warfare. The headbands are also to be seen on warriors of the Solokha treasury, just like the hats.

These bowman include Greeks, since from the 2nd century BC, even Hellenes begin depicting themselves as archers on their gravestones. This is the only case in the Greek world aside from Crete, and as Greeks, many of them add a linothorax to the usual panoply (Cf. Mielczarek 1999 Pl. IX; pp. 50-52).

Two stelae by Bosporan Greeks from the 2nd and 1st century respectively depict horsemen with thureos shields - a unit unlocked by an easily achievable reform (Kieseritzky, G./Watzinger, C., Griechische Grabreliefs aus Südrussland, Berlin (1909), fig. 591 & 688).

The general's bodyguard represents the elite of the (Ionian) Greeks in the realm and is thus well armoured while riding the best horses available.

Xystophoroi, too, are a common Hellenic unit and not too much needs to be said on them.

The *epilektoi* ("chosen ones") are the personal guard cavalry of the king and described as fulfilling a similar role to Alexander's hetairoi by Diodorus (Diod. 20.22.5).

By the 1st century AD, the heavily armoured cataphracts with horse armour going down to the knees, cheires for legs and arms of the riders appear on various wall paintings and stelae (Mielczarek 1999, pl. XV, XXI, XXIII, XXIV).

Sindian mounted archers are the fastest and most agile cavalry of the former Sindian kingdom, and their round shields give them some extra protection against enemy arrows. The shields are based on the toy for a shield found in a Sindian grave (Agrafonov/Spiridonova/Tchekanova, Severnoye Prichernomor'yev antichnuyu epokhu: arkheologiya i mifologiya (2006), fig. 14.8).

The Maiotians, too, have horse archers, and some of them wear rawhide cuirasses found in kurgans in the area (Mielczarek 1999, p. 53).

Finally, Scythian horse archers! As Red Zed would say: "What more do you want?" Most of them, too, wear cuirasses to protect themselves against enemy missile fire.
