Cappadocia

2026-10-31 · ahowl11

Disclaimer - This Dev diary was written by @Mausolos of Mylasa our lead historian, I am simply posting it. All screens are taken by the wonderful @Lollygagging

Welcome back to another dev diary! Today, we are going to present one of the major faction in Anatolia: Cappadocia.

Cappadocia was (is) a large region in eastern Anatolia, stretching from the mighty Tauros mountains in the south to the swampy shores of the Black Sea in the north, from the reddish Halys river and the Great Salt Lake/Lake Tatta in the west to rugged Kommagene and the upper Euphrates in the East (Cf. Strab. 12.1.1C533). Therefore, it was an important part of the Achaemenid Empire, whose great kings controlled it through an elaborate system of fortresses and royal estates (Canepa, The Iranian Expanse (2020), p. 108). When Alexander conquered the empire, he left little impact on Cappadocia, but his actions led to the permanent division of the land into Greater Cappadocia in the south and Cappadocia on the Pontos, later only known as Pontos, in the north. This is explained by the historian and geographer Strabo of Amaseia (ca. 63 BC-25AD), himself from Pontos, in his Geography:

“Cappadocia was divided into two satrapies by the Persians at the time when it was taken over by the Macedonians; the Macedonians willingly allowed one part of the country, but unwillingly the other, to change to kingdoms instead of satrapies; and one of these kingdoms they named “Cappadocia Proper” and “Cappadocia near Tauros” and even “Greater Cappadocia”, and the other they named “Pontos”, though others named it Pontic Cappadocia.” (Strab. 12.1.4 C534).

The kings of Cappadocia Strabo mentioned are the Ariarathids, who had originally been Achaemenid satraps. Their rise is related by Diodorus of Agyrion (ca. 90-30 BC), a contemporary of Strabo:

“At about this time, Alexander of Macedon defeated and overthrew the Persians, and then died; Perdikkas, who at this point held the supreme command, dispatched Eumenes⁠ (of Kardia) to be military governor of Cappadocia. Ariarathes (I) was defeated, and fell in battle,⁠ and Cappadocia itself and the neighbouring regions fell to the Macedonians. Ariarathes (II), the son of the late king, regarding the situation as hopeless for the present, retired with a few followers to Armenia. Not long after, Eumenes and Perdikkas having died, and Antigonos and Seleukos being elsewhere engaged, he obtained an army from Ardoates, king of Armenia, slew Amyntas, the Macedonian general, expelled the Macedonians from the land in short order, and recovered his original domain.” (Diod. 31.19.4-5)

The Ariarathid kingdom of Cappadocia originally encompassed 8 “Strategies” (provinces): Cappadocian Kilikia, Tyanitis, Garsauritis, Laviansene, Sargarausene, Saravene, Chamanene and Morimene. To this, Ariarathes II and Ariarathes III (280-220 BC) added the valuable Kataonia and Melitene (Strobel, Karl, s.v. Kappadokia, in: DNP online). The capital and largest city was Mazaka, later Caesarea, today accordingly known as Kayseri (Sofou, Strabo and the Historical Geography of Cappadocia (2005), p. 760).

Cappadocia cointained much land for grazing horses, cattle and sheep, but aside from Mazaka, there were few cities. Thus it was never the wealthiest realm, but also difficult to control for outsiders. The Ariarathids ruled the land through strategical fortresses, good connections to the temple states (chief of them the temple state of Ma in Komana) and a mutually beneficial relationship with the nobility (Gabelko, Bithynia and Cappadocia (2017), pp. 329-330). Their last monarch was Ariarathes VIII, who came to the throne with the help of the Bithynian kingdom, but was defeated and driven out by Mithridates VI of Pontos in 96/95 BC. Since the Romans opposed Mithridates, they supported the establishment of a new dynasty, named Ariobazarnid after their newly-installed puppet. Ariobarzanes I, however, was exiled by Mithridates or even the Cappadocian nobles so often that I lost count (somewhere between 15 and 30 times). The last Ariobarzanid king was Ariarathes X (very confusing, I know), who was killed by Mark Antony in 36 BC. Mark Antony then promoted Archelaos, the son of the High Priest of Komana and one of Antony's mistresses, to become the last king of Cappadocia. He would rule the kingdom for a whopping 53 years until his death in old age, when Cappadocia became a Roman province (though parts of it were later given to other Roman client kingdoms).

The Starting Position of Cappadocia

The first unit are the Cappadocian temple servants. They represent the followers of the temple states of Cappadocia and Pontos. The most significant are the two Komanas, devoted to Ma, the goddess of bloody melee. They are not well armed, but they are madmen who would die to defend their sanctuary

The levy spearmen are poorly trained and armed spearmen from the villages of Cappadocia, who fight with spears and wooden shields, but not much else.

Cappadocian Light Infantry is based on an account by the Greek historian Herodotus (7.72.1), who says they wore woven helmets, small shields, shorts spears and javelins

The royal infantry guard, as the name suggests, is a vastly superior unit and reprents, well, the guard of the rulers of Cappadocia, who held the title of kings from ca. 250 BC. They also reflect the influences of the diadoch Eumenes of Kardia, who recruited Cappadocian soldiers into his Hellenistic army in 322/321 BC (Plutarch, Life of Eumenes 4.3-4)

Like the light infantry, the Cappadocian javelinmen are based on the passage by Herodotus who adds some only wore daggers, and with the javelins, they could obviously fight as skirmishers

Cappadocian slingers are attested by the finds of lead bullets now in the Yozgat Museum in western Cappadocia (Buora/Lafli, Five inscribed lead instrumenta from Turkey, in: Baratta, Instrumenta Inscripta VIII (2021), fig. 1-4. Dated Hellenistic period (Buora/Lafli 2021, 237).

Cappadocian archers are depicted on the coinage of Ariarathes I, satrap of Cappadocia under the Persians and then its first independent ruler until 322 BC (SNG Cop 129).

Cappadocian light cavalry is basically the mounted version of the light infantry. It would have been an important part of the army since light horsemen were also ever present in the Achaemenid army and Cappadocia was strongly shaped by the Persians.

The early bodyguard of Cappadocian commanders is based on Persian cavalry as well. The elite of the Achaemenids already used front armour for the horses and cuirasses for the riders. Some of them wear the huge all-round leather cuirass seen on the Çan sarcophagus from Mysia (ca. 400-375 BC)

The late general's bodyguard, meanwhile, is a cataphract unit due to the prevalence of cataphracts in the late Cappadocian army - based on influences from Parthia and the Seleucids.

Cappadocian cavalry was famous and is often depicted on Cappadocian coins. Well armoured and with long lances, they combine equipment previously seen and can deliver a lethal charge.

The noble cavalry is a reform unit which is close to the early bodyguard unit and more heavily armoured than the normal Cappadocian Cavalry unit. Again, the cuirass from the Çan sarcophagus and the armour frontlets for horses appear.

Even heavier are the Cappadocian cataphracts. Everybody knows and fears these warriors, and with late Cappadocia being sandwhiched between Seleucids, Armenia and Parthians, it is no surprise they also introduced this style of super heavy shock cavalry.

Your earliest reform unit, however, are the Cappadocian Thureophoroi, who are based on the description of Cappadocian infantry at the battle of Magnesia in 190 BC. The Roman historian Livy (Liv. 37.40.9) claims they fought in the same style as the Galatian footmen next to them, and therefore they received thureos shields, swords and javelins.

Cappadocian chariots appear on a frieze in the Yozgat Museum. This traditional near eastern weapon can cause a lot of havoc if used correctly and can only be found in the roster of very few factions.

The Cappadocian skirmisher cavalry is a mounted version of the Cappadocian javelinmen and thus a dedicated skirmisher version of the light cavalry.

Finally, a reform gives you the heavy skirmisher cavalry, Which is attested by the so-called 'Roman' frieze at the Yozgat Museum, on which they appear wearing pelte shields, linothorakes, kopis swords, Armenian conical helmets and javelins.
