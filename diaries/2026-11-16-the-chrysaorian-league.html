The Chrysaorian League

2026-11-16 · ahowl11

Disclaimer - This Dev diary was written by @Mausolos of Mylasa our lead historian, I am simply posting it. All screens are taken by the wonderful @Vas & @Lollygagging

Chairete, philoi, and welcome back to the RIS dev diaries!

Our next faction is the CHRYSAORIAN LEAGUE of the Carians. First, a word on the name because you may wonder why our Carian faction is not called Caria or Carians. "Chrysaor" means "the one with the golden sword", and this was the Carian name for Zeus in Stratonikeia, where the federal temple of the Chrysaorian League, a religious federation of (almost) all Carians, was located. Hence, Chrysaoria was also used as a secondary name for Caria, and in mythology, there was also a hero called Chrysaor, the brother of the famous Bellerophon, who was supposed to be the founder of Caria (Steph. Byz. s.v. Chrysaoris; Hes. Theog. 280-283). Regardless, the Carians primarily revered Zeus, who also had a second, equally important sanctuary in Labraunda, a few kilometres north of the old capital of the region, Mylasa (Ma, Antiochos III and the Cities of Western Asia Minor (2000), p. 69).

The double axe of Zeus Labraundos, the Labrys (Plutarch claims Labraunda was named after the axe: Plut. Quaest. Graec. XIV.2.302a), was the sacred symbol of the Carians, and they also appreciated the Labyrs and other axes in battle. Together with the drepanon, a scythe-like melee weapon, they differentiated Carian warriors from Greeks, with whom they had close contacts early on (Hdt. 7.93). According to Herodotus, the first mercenaries ever were Ionian Greeks and Carians in Egypt around 600 BC (Hdt. 2.152.4; cf. Polyaen. 7.3). The father of history, himself born in the Dorian colony Halikarnassos in coastal Caria, adds that the Carians invented handles on shields and the practice of painting shields and putting crests on helmets (Hdt. 1.171.4). Though it is doubtful if this is true, it certainly speaks for the Carians' reputation in warfare. At the same time, mercenaries were also expendable: Polybios later wrote that if a situation on a campaign seemed dangerous, one would "send the Carians" - this became a common proverb among Greek officers (Polyb. 10.32.11).

Caria had been settled since the Bronze Age but never formed a united political unit. Intensive political, military, economic, cultural and religious contacts between Greeks and Carians shaped both groups throughout the centuries, and these persisted even when Caria fell first under Lydian and then Persian rule in the 6th century BC. Carians fought for and with Miletos during the Ionian Revolt (499-494 BC), and the local ruler, Artemisia I, impressed Xerxes with her bravery at the battle of Salamis in 480 BC (Hdt. 7.99; 8.68-69; 87-88) - a scene made famous by the poor sequel of 300, where Artemisia is played by Eva Green. Artemisia II, the wife and sister of Mausolos, the mightiest satrap of Caria, is more important and generally well-known. Under his rule in the mid-4th century BC, the satrapy of Caria became one of the richest and most influential in the whole Achaemenid Empire, with various Greek cities, Rhodes, and Lycia falling under Mausolos' rule, and other regional powers like Chios or Kos accepting his hegemony (Hornblower, Mausolus (1982)). Mausolos 'hellenised' the Carians and 'carianised' the Greeks in his lands, and he moved the capital from inland Mylasa to the thriving port of Halikarnassos. Here, he began the construction of the gigantic Mausoleum, named after him, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world. Many buildings since have been called mausoleums, and many structures have emulated the original architectural design - the house of the temple in Washington, D.C., would be one example.

Mausolos was part of the Hekatomnid dynasty, which began with Hekatomnos in the late 5th century BC but may have been connected to Artemisia I. After Mausolos' death, his sister Artemisia II succeeded him, finished the construction of the Mausoleum and defended his realm against Rhodian attacks. Several decades later, when Alexander the Great came to Halikarnassos, Ada, the last of the Hekatomnids, proclaimed herself to be Alexander's aunt and received his support (even though the claim was nonsense) (Arr. Anab. 1.23; 2.5; Diod. 16.69.2; 17.24.2). After Alexander's campaigns, however, Caria passed into the hands of various diadochi before ending up under Ptolemaic control. They ruled the region mostly indirectly, with only a few garrisons here and there (Cohen, The Hellenistic Settlements in Europe, the Islands, and Asia Minor (1996), pp. 52-54). The Chrysaorian League, as a religious and loose political federation, bound Stratonikeia, Alabanda, Alinda, Mylasa, Kaunos and others together and was open to Ptolemaic influence. At the end of the 3rd century, Antiochos III and Philip V conquered Caria for the Seleucids and Antigonids, respectively. Still, they retained the league and arbitrated in its conflicts with the polis of Mylasa. Alabanda was even renamed "Antiocheia of the Chrysaorians" by Antiochos (Ma (2000), p. 175). After the Seleucid defeat at Magnesia in 190/189 BC, the region was given to Rhodes, and the Rhodians attempted to control Caria directly. The Carians seemingly utilised the Chrysaorian League for conspirative meetings, however, and started a revolt, which eventually won acknowledgement from the Senate and led to the liberation of most of Caria in the late 170s and early 160s BC (Gruen, The Hellenistic World (1986), p. 571). Even when the Rhodians defeated Mylasa and Alabanda decisively, the Romans did not allow them to keep their gains - not least because Alabanda had sent cavalry shields to support the Roman campaigns in Macedon (Liv. 43.6.6).

After Caria had finally gained independence, the Chrysaorian League remained a religious and social confederation for all Carians, without always being able to bring them together politicially. The Koinon (Greek for league) survived into the imperial period, when Aphrodisias, the latest famous Carian cult centre, received much attention from Augustus and became one of the most decorated cities in the empire (Thommen, “The Sebasteion at Aphrodisias", in: Chronika 2 (2012), pp. 82-91). Still, the individual *poleis* and communities instead favoured to remain autonomous from each other most of the time. In RIS, this may, of course, change: The Chrysaorian League can spawn during your campaign and possibly unite Caria, but in truth, it is more likely that Caria will often be split between the Chrysaorians and a host of foreign powers - just like in history.

The Chrysaorian League revolts itself into the game, but you can just click them in the start menu and you will spawn in after one turn. From there on, you can unite Karia!

The Light Infantry is the first unique unit for the Chrysaorians - even though it is, in true Carian fashion, widely available as a mercenary unit for other Hellenistic factions. The light armour and devastating labrys double axes - the symbol of Zeus Labraundos, the Zeus of Mylasa and the other main god of Caria besides Chrysaor - make them a light and swift assault unit.

Our Carian faction also uses a lot of Greek units which you know already. Due to the intense relations between Carians and Greeks since the earliest times, many Carians would have been virtually indistinguishable from Greeks. We spoke about Herodotus' account of Carian and Ionian mercenaries in Egypt above, and it is pretty clear that for the Egyptians there was no difference between Ionian Greeks and Carians. Homer calls the Carians barbarophonoi ("sounding like barbarians"), but that was probably because, in his time, they were the only non-Greeks to even speak Greek - as was already pointed out by various Greek authors (Thuc. 1.3.3; Strab. 14.2.28C661-662).

The Karian Thureophoroi are based on the (many) Carians in Ptolemaic service, where thureos shields had become widespread by the later 3rd century BC. One of those mercenaries, Stomphias, son of Apollodides, from Euromos near Mylasa, wore the red striped tunics and the Sidon helmets seen here (Jalabert, Nouvelles Stèles Peintes de Sidon (1904), fig. 1).

The red colours as well as the gold of Chrysaor and the characteristically white shields of Caria (Xen. Hell. 3.2.15) inspired the design of the Carian Hoplites, who also include the Dorian Greeks of Halikarnassos, Kos and Knidos. Like other hoplites, they are a reliable option to hold your line.

Our Karian Heavy Infantry is similar to the hoplites, but fights with the scythe-like drepanon mentioned above. With its iron blade and curved shape, the drepanon is a terrible armour-piercing weapon. Use these men to charge the enemy while your hoplites remain in defense.

The Leukaspides ("White Shields") are based on Xenophon's claim that Carians fought with white shields (Xen. Hell. 3.2.15) and the central role of the Chryaorion, the federal sanctuary of the league, which would have been defended at all costs. Besides the drepanon, they use the omnipresent double-edged, straight Greek xiphos. As the best infantry unit in your roster, their strength, too, lies in attack.

The Neocretan archers are Carian bowmen trained by mercenary Cretans - the best archers in the Mediterranean world. The Roman historian Livy mentions them in the Seleucid army at Magnesia and there is no reason why the Chrysaorian League would have disbanded these excellent heavy archers (Liv. 37.40.12).

The Alabandine Aspidophoroi, finally, are the unique Carian cavalry unit. In 170 BC, Alabanda sent 300 cavalry aspides to the Romans to support their campaign against the Antigonids and gain the senate's support for Alabanda's own fight against Rhodes (Liv. 43.6.6). This certainly suggests that in Caria, as elsewhere in the Greek world, the roundshield had been adopted by horsemen. Aspidophoroi are an excellent dual-purpose heavy cavalry and round off the roster.
