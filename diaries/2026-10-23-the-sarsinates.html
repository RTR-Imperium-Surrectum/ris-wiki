The Sarsinates

2026-10-23 · jottel

Hello everyone, and welcome to our new Italian Developer Diary. Today, we present to you the Sarsinates. They are an obscure Umbrian community that got conquered in the early 260s BC, one of the last campaigns conducted in Italy by the Romans before the First Punic War. Information on them -as on the Umbrians as a whole - is quite sparse. So, what do we know? Sarsina was an Umbrian city (Strab. V.2.10) on the Sapis river (modern Savio). In the Fasti Triumphales, the year 266 BC contains the triumphs of the consuls D. Junius Pera and Num. Fabius Pictor over the “Sassinates” (a common alternate version of the name in Latin sources). In the Roman and Italian census of 225 BC, the “Umbrians and Sarsinates inhabiting he Apennines” (mentioned separately) have a manpower of 20.000 men (Polyb. II.24.7). This might imply their significance as a separate people from the remaining Umbrians. They have good cheese (Plin. NH. XI.97.1; Mart. I.43.7; III.58.35) Their land is rich in flocks (Sil. Ital. VIII.461) There is a sacred lake with a local nymph to whom Caesius Sabinus of Sarsina (friend of Martialis) dedicated a temple (Mart. IX.58). And the Roman poet Plautus came from there (Hier. Chron. B200). That … is basically it from the written sources. Archaeologically, we can add that it was founded in the second half of the 4th century BC, and by then was essentially the only “settlement of higher order” that was founded in Umbria at the time (Sewell: Higher-order Settlements in Early Hellenistic Italy (2016), p. 621). It had a sacred district from the 3rd century BC, and the Romans constructed a grid of parallel streets, public buildings at the forum and a domus (Morciano, Maria Milvia: Sarsina, in: DNP 12 (2008), p. 1003).

If we expand the category to the Umbrians, we can glean a little bit more. Here we shall start with the origins of the Umbrians. There are essentially two rival versions, mentioned by the 1st century BC author Dionysios of Halikarnassos: The first is usually the less favoured and has the Umbrians as natives of Italy alongside the Etruscans (Gr: *tyrrhenoi*), their name deriving from the forts (Gr: *tyrseis*) which they inhabited (Dion. Hal. I.26.2). The parallel version, favoured by Herodotus, has the Etruscans come from a Lydian colonial expedition under the leadership of a prince called Tyrrhenos. (Hdt. I.94; Dion. Hal. I.27). They displaced the Umbrians from their native lands and expanded throughout Italy (Hdt. I.94; Strab. V.1.10; Plin. NH. III.50). On the basis that the historian Xanthos of Lydia (5th century BC) did not mention that tradition, as well as the apparent language differences between Lydians and Etruscans, Dionysios rejects it (Dion. Hal. I.28.2; I.30.1). Either way, the nativity of the Umbrians in Italy remains unquestioned in both viewpoints. Marcus Antonius (preserved in Solin. II.11), claimed that the Umbrians descended from the ancient Gauls. According to him, they survived the great mythical catastrophic flood and were therefore called “Umbrians” by the Greeks, from ὄμβρος/ombros, “shower/rain.” In the account of Dionysios, the Oinotrians—immigrant Greeks from Arkadia—seized part of Umbrian territory and thereafter called themselves Aborigines (Dion. Hal. I.13.3; I.16.1; I.19.1). He also records early campaigns by Pelasgians and Aborigines against the Umbrians, including the capture of “Krotona” (probably Cortona, later an Etruscan city), which the Aborigines fortified against the Umbrians. Citing Zenodotos of Troizen (ca. 3rd c. BC), they also inhabited the territory around Reate and after being driven out by the Pelasgians, they – or this section of the Umbrians – called themselves Sabines (Dion. Hal. II.49.1).

Umbria was located along the eastern boundary of Etruria, across the Apennines and initially extending from the Po River to the Adriatic coast (Strab. V.2.10, Steph. Byz. O492). For the 4th century BC, Umbrians are still attested around the Greek colonies of Ankon and Spina, where they may have expelled the Siculi and Liburni, before they themselves were displaced by “Pelasgians” and Etruscans (Scyl. I.16-17, Just. XX.1, Plin. NH III.50; III.112). Information about their territorial extent is given to us in a very fragmentary state, mainly from Greek geographers and historians of the 4th and 3rd century BC. Their works only survive in the citation of others, and their contact to actual Umbrians can be questioned, like Theopompos, who simply said that they enjoyed a luxurious, effeminate lifestyle comparable to the Lydians (Jacoby, FGrH 2 B 115, repeated by Pseudo-Scymnus 220 – 221 and Pseudo-Aristotelian De mirabilibus Auscultationibus (80)). According to Strabo, the Umbrians and Etruscans often fought over questions of precedence. Separated only by the Tiber, each tried to match an invasion with an equal counter-invasion, though the Etruscans won out in the end, overall taking 300 settlements of the Umbrians (Strab. V.1.10). They drove the Umbrians from the Po Valley, which the latter subsequently attempted to recover. The famous tablets recovered from the Umbrian city Iguvium likewise reflect the hostility between the two peoples, as representatives of both Etruscans and Iapygians were to be expelled before the rite of lustrating the army (Tabulae Iguvinae Ib 16‒17). Both Etruscans and Umbrians also rivalled each other in their foundation of numerous colonies, though according to Strabo, the Umbrians founded more than the Etruscans, demonstrated by settlements in Cisalpina. Livy preserves a similar tradition, claiming that during the first Celtic migration into the Po Valley, the newcomers expelled *both* the Etruscans and the Umbrians (Liv. V.35.1).

The aforementioned connection of the Umbrians to the Sabines can be easily traced through the so-called “Sabellic”/Osco-Umbrian languages, which include Marrucinian, Marsian, Oscan, Paelignian, South Picene, Sabine, Umbrian, Vestinian and Volscian (maybe Hernician and “Pre-Samnite”). The harsh mountainous environment and the attraction of the fertile coastal regions probably encouraged their common ancestors to move into the valleys and through repeated “sacred springs,” the Osco-Umbrian peoples subsequently splintered into various groups. In times of hardship, they dedicated part of their produce to Mars, armed all the men born in a particular year, and sent them out under favourable auspices to establish a colony (Dion. Hal. I.16.2, Strab. V.4.12). These groups usually followed a sacred animal. The Sabines who followed a woodpecker (*picus*), a bird that is sacred to Mars, called themselves Picentes (Strab. V.4.2, Plin. NH. III.110). At Matiene in the Apennines stood an ancient oracle of Mars, comparable to Dodona, where the sacred *picus* delivered prophecies. The Romans also adopted the woodpecker as a guiding animal, as a *picus* fed and watched over the infants Romulus and Remus while they were suckled by a she-wolf (Plut. Rom. IV.2). Italic tradition also names Picus as the mythical king of the Aborigines (Fest. 228.32-34) who is later transformed into a woodpecker by Circe (Verg. Aen. VII.189-192). Other examples include the Samnites who were sent out during a Sabine war with the Umbrians and followed a bull, whereas the Hirpini followed a wolf (Strab. V.4.12). A related motif appears in the myth of Aeneas: before arriving on the Italian coast from Troy, he received an oracle instructing him to follow a four-footed animal as his guide, which proved to be a pregnant sow (Dion Hal. I.55.4). He followed it for 24 stades until it collapsed, then he sacrificed it to his household gods and founded Lavinium on that spot (Dion Hal. I.56 – 1.57.1).

Towards the lower Tiber River, Sabellic groups identifying under the Sabine (*safin-*) name probably penetrated Latium by the 8th century BC, as Roman tradition appears to preserve. Romulus’ “abduction” of the Sabine women and the ensuing wars with the Sabine king Titus Tatius (Liv. I.9-10), which ended with the mutual agreement of shared kingship between Romulus and Tatius, may reflect a process of interaction and integration between Latin and Sabine populations during this period. The Romans thought that a lot of their terminology originated from the Sabines, as attested in Varro’s Lingua Latina, while prominent families such as the Valerii and Claudii claimed Sabine ancestry. The 6th and especially the 5th century BC also saw the emergence of the Volsci, Aequi, and Hernici in Latium, peoples whom Rome confronted for much of the next two centuries (Liv. I.53). Their rapid occupation of much of Latium in the late 6th or early 5th century BC could be tied to conductions of sacred springs. For most of the 5th century BC, the Volsci controlled the Monti Lepini, the Pomptine Plain, and the Latin coast from Antium to Terracina. By 495 BC, they had occupied Cora and Pometia (Liv. II.22.2); Antium had fallen before 493 BC (Liv. II.23.4), and Velitrae shortly thereafter. Cornell suggests that the absence of major Latin towns such as Praeneste, Tibur, and Pedum from the historical narratives for this period may likewise imply their subjugation by Aequian invaders (Cornell: The Beginnings of Rome (1995), p. 306). Rome fought against the Volsci and Aequi from ca. 500 BC until the Samnite Wars in the late 4th century BC, by which point most of the Volsci cities had been colonized and the Aequi essentially destroyed.

During the Samnite wars, Rome penetrated deeper into the Apennine areas, for the first time in 340 BC, when they marched through Marsic territory to reach Capua (Liv. VIII.6.8). In 304 BC, after the Aequi were almost exterminated and an alliance of Apennine tribes defeated, Rome established a friendship treaty with the Marsi, Marrucini, Paeligni and Frentani (Liv. IX.45.18). A year later, Rome encroached on them with the foundation of the colony of Alba (Fucens) in Aequi territory, close to the border of the Marsi (Liv. X.1.1-2), and in 298 BC, the Marsi rose up against the foundation of the colony at Carseoli, also in Aequian territory (X.12.1). They were routed and their fortified towns Milionia, Plestina and Freselia taken in just a few days (Liv. X.3.5-6). Besides the construction of the Via Valeria, cutting through the territories of the Marsi and Paeligni to reach the Adriatic, further Roman encroachments remained very light (Fronda, Between Rome and Carthage (2010), p. 292-293). Likely, Rome was more interested in the manpower the inhabitants of the Apennines could provide through their respective alliance treaties compared to any territory they could offer. Thus, the Apennine tribes became probably the most loyal peoples to Rome until the Social War (91-88 BC). An inscription of Caso Cantovios on a looted bronze belt dedicated to the Marsic goddess Angitia (CIL I2 5), dated to 294 BC, demonstrates that the Marsi had likely already participated in the battle of Sentinum in 295 BC on the Roman side. It was captured “near the Gallic boundary” (*apur finem calicom* = *apud finem Galli cum*), written in Latin script, and makes use both of Latin onomastic formulae and of Roman military terms (lecionibus) (Letta: The Marsi, in: Bradley, Farney: The Peoples of Italy (2018), p. 513).

Peoples of these parts of Italy appeared in various wars and battles on the Roman side: Frentani, Umbri, Volsci, Marrucini and Paeligni at the battle of Herakleia (280 BC, Dion. Hal. XX.1.5; Plut. Pyrrh. XVI.9), twenty thousand foot and four thousand horse of Marsi, Marrucini, Frentani, and Vestini at the census of 225 BC, contingents of Marsi, Paeligni, and Marrucini as volunteers for Scipio’s fleet in 205 BC (Liv. XXVIII.45), Marrucini, Paeligni, Vestini and Samnites at the battle of Pydna (Liv. XL.40), and Paeligni throwing their standards into enemy formations to induce their men to charge during the Second Punic and Third Macedonian War (Liv. XXV.14; Plut. Aem. XX.1).

Ironically, the *Umbrians* themselves became very obscure, probably because the “Umbrian” communities stayed rather independent from one another without greater cooperation, as demonstrated by the few inscriptions we have (Bradley: Umbria from the Iron Age to the Augustan Era (2016), p. 144). Many authors recognised the antiquity of ancestral Umbrian traditions, but for the Romans, the 4th and 3rd centuries BC confrontations with *the* Umbrian communities were barely worth noting. An alliance treaty was concluded with Camerinum in 310 BC (Liv. IX.36.6), which remained on equal terms even up to 205 BC, when Scipio Africanus needed men for his expedition to Africa (XXVIII.45). In the same year (310 BC), an Umbrian army was defeated, mentioned in an off-handed sentence (IX.39.4). Two years later, a pan-Umbrian army (probably excluding Camerinum) that was scraped together and had assembled at Mevania was so easily dealt with that the Roman could afford to knock most soldiers unconscious with shield bashes rather than kill them (Liv. IX.41). The Umbrian communities surrendered and the community of Ocriculum concluded a friendship treaty (Liv. IX.41.20). In 300 BC, the Umbrian city Nequinum was taken and made into the colony Narnia (Liv. X.10). Like the Etruscans, the Umbrians had initially planned to participate in the large battle at Sentinum (which was in Umbria) but for some unexplained reason, they did not (Liv. X.21.2; X.27.11). For 267 BC, the submission of the Umbrians and Sallentines is noted in Livy’s Periochae (Liv. Per. 15e), and in 266 BC, the two consuls of the year celebrated a triumph over the Sarsinates. Their separate mention in the formula togatorum of 225 BC implies that they were either seen as separate from the Umbrians or had a special status, perhaps due to their isolation in the north (Polyb. II.24.7).

In the year 241/40 BC; the Romans founded the lofty colony of Spoletum, which Hannibal Barca failed to take while ravaging through Umbria in 217 BC (Liv. Per. XXe; Liv. XX.9). Silius Italicus, trying to mirror the Latin army catalogue written by his personal idol Vergilius (Verg. Aen. VII. 647-817), mentions the various Italian communities on Rome’s side during the battle of Cannae (216 BC). These also include Umbrian ones who stand out in terms of their geographical traits, like pastures (Arna, Mevania, Sil. VIII.456), ruggedness (Hispellum, Narnia, VIII. 457-458), miscellaneous features (Iguvium, Fulginia, VIII. 459-460), their bellicosity (Amerians, Camertes, Tuder, VIII.461-462) or their “rich flocks” (Sassina VIII.461). For Scipio Africanus’ campaign in 205 BC, the Umbrian communities, as well as Nursia, Reate and Amiternum promised to furnish men, and Camerinum sent a cohort of 600 men (Liv. XXVIII.45). This pretty much summarizes their appearances in Roman historical events. During the Social War (91-89 BC), the Umbrians revolted along with the Etruscans, though they either were quickly pacified with the offer of citizenship (App. BC. I.6.49) or defeated (Liv. Per. 74e).

Since the DD focused on the origin of various Sabellian peoples which may have sprung from the Umbrians, we will quickly cover the Area-of-Recruitment units that you will find in the Apennine area.

Marsi swordsmen are a unit from the very bellicose peoples based around their metropolis Marruvium. Their status as snake charmers was widely regarded and thus, the snake symbol appears on some of their shields. As mentioned above, their presence at Sentinum in 295 BC can be asserted, as well as their support of Rome during the Second Punic War. The Social War was often called the “Marsic War” in the sources, as the Marsi were one of the main factions involved in the rebellion until 6.000 of them were slain in battle by Sulla. Appian himself says that “no triumph was ever awarded for a victory over them except for this single disaster. There had been up to this time a saying, ‘No triumph over Marsians or without Marsians.’” (App. BC. I.6.46). This makes them one of the best sword units in Italy.

Marrucinian Spearmen represent the Marrucinian peoples whose capital was at Teanum. They were already present in the Roman army at the battle of Herakleia 280 BC. Their contingents also appeared as volunteers to Scipio’s fleet in 205 BC and at the battle of Pydna 168 BC, where they and the Paeligni suffered the majority of losses for throwing themselves at the Macedonian phalanx. A little statuette of a spearmen was found in their territory at Chieti.

The Vestini are represented via axemen. Silius Italicus says about them that they were “inferior to none as fighters, and hardened by hunting wild animals, came in serried ranks; their flocks graze on the heights of Fiscellus and green Pinna, and in the meadows of Aveia that are quick to grow again." (Sil. VIII.515)

We represented the Paeligni and their famous standard-throwers with light infantry. Among all Apennine tribes, they probably have the most numerous appearances as cohorts in the Roman army: at Herakleia (280 BC), at Beneventum, where they threw their standard into Hanno’s camp to create an impetus for a charge (212 BC), at Pydna where they did the same (168 BC), or in Africa during the Jugurthine War (105 BC). They were also seen as the initiators of the Social War along with the Picentes and Marsi (Eutr. V.3).

Frentanian Cavalry appear in the Roman army at the battle of Herakleia (280 BC) under their commander Oplax, who directly attacks Pyrrhos and kills the Epeirote king’s horse. Their coinage from Larinum also represents shielded heavy cavalry.
