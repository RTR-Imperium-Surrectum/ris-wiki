The Lucanians and Bruttians

2026-10-10 · jottel

Hello everyone, and welcome to our new Italian Developer Diary. Today, we present to you the Lucanians and Bruttians, two closely related Oscan peoples who created a polity independent from the Samnites in the 5th - 4th century BC. The Lucanians took over several Italiote cities in Southern Italy like Poseidonia in ca. 410 BC and Laos and Skidros ca. 390 BC. In the middle of the 4th century BC, “Leukania” is still known by Pseudo-Skylax to begin from Samnite land, starting at Poseidonia and reaching down to Rhegion, including the cities Elea, Laos, Pandosia, Klampeteia, Terina, Hipponion and Medma (Scyl. I.12). Though the conquest of these cities was likely generally violent, excavations at Poseidonia point to a peaceful takeover, as no burnt layer was excavated. A joint Lucanian-Poseidonian assault on Elea may additionally reflect a beneficial partnership between Poseidonian Greeks and Lucanians rather than a violent oppression; more akin to Neapolis rather than Capua (Isayev, Inside Ancient Lucania (2007), p. 112-114; Strab. VI.1.1). Their proximity to the Southern Greeks may explain why “Lucanian” has occasionally been used to refer to Samnite peoples overall, as implied in the (Pseudo-)Aristotelean Mirabilia, where the author describes Kyme (Cumae) as being under Lucanian control instead of Campanian (Arist. Mir. 95). They are also mentioned by Isocrates, who uses them to make a point about peoples that don’t “share their ignoble origin” with other peoples (Isoc. 8.50). Lucanian inscriptions during the 4th century BC point to an adoption of Greek script and vocabulary for bureaucratic purposes. Thus, Greek formulas or terms like “archon” (found at Serra di Vaglio) were in use for official purposes, next to known Oscan ones, like “meddix” (at Muro Lucano) (Isayev (2007), p. 127-128). According to Strabo, the Lucanian government was overall democratic, though in times of war, a king (*basileus*) would be elected (Strab. VI.1.3).

Animal husbandry and trade over mountain ridges were the most important economic branches of the Lucanian economy (Isayev (2007); p. 48-52). Though Lucania also had some fertile valleys for agriculture, it wasn’t noteworthy enough for ancient sources to mention. The hilly sites were more suitable for vines, and at the time of Pliny, the Lucanian vintages (among others) were more popular than those of Thurioi (Isayev (2007), p. 52; Plin. NH. XIV.69). More than that, thick forests stretched from Lucania into Bruttium. The inhabitants of the latter had to give up half of their districts of the Sila forest after submitting to the Romans in the 270s BC. It is from that wood that the Romans probably produced a large part of their fleet during the First Punic War (Dion. Hal. XX.15; Isayev (2007), p. 52-53). In the forests of Lucania, boar hunting was a widely known and celebrated activity (Hor. Sat. II.3.233-6; Diod. IV.22.3-4; Stat. Theb. VIII.529-35). From the boar meat, Lucanians made sausages, which the Romans accordingly also called “Lucanica” as they first saw it among them (Varr. LL. V.111; Cic. Fam. IX.16; Mart. IV.46; Isayev (2007), p. 53). Justin says that the Lucanians brought their children up like the Spartans, keeping them in wilderness among the shepherds during boyhood. They would have had no slaves that attended to them, nor clothes or sleeping places. Thus, they were to get accustomed to hardship and a spare diet, having to hunt and forage their own food (Just. XXIII.1). By the time of Seneca, Roman aristocrats would seek hunting adventures in the Lucanian mountains as a change to the calmness of their villa retreats in Campania (Seneca, De tranquilitate animi II.13; Isayev (2007), p. 53). The coastal areas apparently had a lot of gems and in the imperial period, Paestum was known for its perfumes and Elea for its healing waters, becoming a centre of medicine (Isayev (2007), p. 53). The Lucanians made their first regular appearances in the wars of the tyrant Dionysios I of Syracuse (ca. 432 – 367 BC). The Italiote League was founded for the first time in the 390s BC in opposition to both Dionysios and his Lucanian allies (Diod. XIV.91.1). Thourioi often bore the brunt of Lucanian incursions and once got an entire Italiote army of 15.000 men massacred in a Lucanian ambush (Diod. XIV.101-102). Polyainos is the only one mentioning early appearances of the Lucanians in the 5th century BC, when the Spartan general Kleandridas apparently defeated the Lucanians on several occasions on behalf of the Thourians (Polyaen. II.10.2; II.10.4). In 356 BC, a large group of Lucanian slaves from multiple regions split off from their masters, first marauding the countryside, and then taking over the cities in the toe of Italy, starting with Terina and then Hipponion, Thourioi and Petelia, the “metropolis” of the Chones, one of the rare Southern Italian settlements remaining populous even to the time of Strabo (Strab. VI.1.3). They formed a league, which they called “Brettioi” (Diod. XVI.15). According to Strabo, the Bruttians were named as such by the Lucanians, because they call all rebels “brettii”. He too says that they were slaves at first who tended to the Lucanian flocks, but then rebelled around the time, when Dion made his expedition against Dionysios II (357 BC Strab. VI.1.4). In Justin’s tradition (of Pompeius Trogus, ca. 1st century BC), the Bruttians came about, because Dionysios I of Syracuse had built a Libyan fortress in Italy, which was betrayed to the Lucanians by a woman named Bruttia. Thus, the inhabitants called themselves Bruttians (Just. XXIII.1).

After asserting their independence from the Lucanians in a successful war, they continued working closely together with their former masters, likely because of outside threats. Together they fought Alexander the Molossian (343/2 – 331 BC), who had engaged the tribes in a war on behalf of the Tarentines. He reconquered Herakleia from the Lucanians, who must have taken it at some unspecific time and then followed it up with campaigns into the south (Liv. VIII.24.4). Near the city Pandosia on the river Acheron, he was javelined to death by Lucanians and Bruttians in battle (Liv. VIII.24.12; Just. XII.2). The famous Syracusan tyrant Agathokles (361 – 289 BC) also engaged with the Bruttians and Lucanians in his goal to expand into Italy, though he ended up just making an equal peace with the Italics (Just. XXIII.1). Several conflicts are also recorded between Taras and the Lucanians between 346 BC (Diod. XVI.61.4) and 303 BC, where the city called over Kleonymos of Sparta for help (Diod. XX.104.1). The poet Leonidas of Taras (active in the early 3rd BC) mentions Lucanian spoils hung up in sanctuaries of Taras, among them 8 thureos shields, 8 helmets, 8 “woven armour”, and as many blood-stained kopides, dedicated to Coryphasian Athene by Hagnon, son of Euanthes (Anth. Gr. 6.129). He also mentions Lucanian “great shields”, a row of bridles and double-pointed spears dedicated to Pallas (Anth. Gr. 6.131). Tomb wall painting from Paestum, 4th century BC:

Roman and Lucanian relations initially had a great start. Lucanian and Apulian delegates had made an alliance with the Romans against the Samnites around 330 BC (Liv. VIII.25.3). In 317 BC, the alliance broke down and by the year 298 BC, the Lucanians were subjugated by Scipio Barbatus (Liv. IX.20.9; CIL I? 7 = ILLRP 309; Meiggs, Lewis: Lucani, in: DNP (2005), p. 827). In 285 BC, the city of Thourioi dedicated a statue and golden crown to the tribune C. Aelius for having introduced a law against the Lucanian general Sthennius Stallius who had twice made an attack upon Thourioi (Plin. NH. XXXIV.32). In 280 BC, Taras called over Pyrrhos of Epeiros for their war against Rome, and the Lucanians and Bruttians also allied to him. They promptly picked up their operations against the Italiote cities in the south again that were thwarted two years before, when C. Fabricius Luscinus relieved the city of Thurioi from a Lucanian and Bruttian siege (Dion. Hal. XX.42; Val. Max. I.8.6; Amm. Marc. XXIV.4.24). This siege and the following invasion of Pyrrhos was the reason why Rome dispatched garrisons to the Italiote cities in the south for their protection, whereafter the Campanian garrison of Rhegion massacred the male population of the city they were supposed to protect (Dion. Hal. XX.4.2). Pyrrhos recruited Lucanians and Bruttians into his army though they proved to be unreliable in the middle of the line at the battle of Herakleia (Dion. Hal. XIX-XX). Eight Triumphs against Lucani and Brutti were recorded between 282 and 272 BC in the *fasti triumphales* and both peoples were subjected and brought into the Roman alliance after 272 BC. Tomb wall painting from Paestum, 4th century BC

After several Roman victories over the Lucanians, Paestum became a colony in 273 BC. Unlike the earlier Lucanian takeover, Roman rule is clearly visible archaeologically: the *ekklesiasterion* (the meeting place of the popular assembly) was dismantled and replaced by a Roman sanctuary; a new forum separated the southern sacred area from the repurposed agora; coins adopted new imagery and the Latin name Paestum; and a new territorial system overlaid the archaic road to Hera’s sanctuary on the Sele. Overall, activity at sanctuaries and rural sites in the Poseidonian chora declined during the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC (Isayev (2007), p. 116-117). In the Roman and Italic manpower census of 225 BC, the Lucanians reportedly supplied 30,000 infantry and 3,000 cavalry, while the Bruttians were not listed (Polyb. II.24.12). The Lucanians initially supported Rome in the Second Punic War: shortly before Cannae in 216 BC, a Lucanian cavalry squadron under Marius Statilius served as scouts (Liv. XXII.42). After the battle, however, many Lucanians and Bruttians joined Hannibal and supplied much of his later manpower (Liv. XXII.61). In 214 BC, however, Sempronius Gracchus still raised Lucanian cohorts (Liv. XXIV.20) and evidently trusted his Lucanian ally Flavus, who led him into an ambush (Liv. XXV.16). After repeated defeats, the Lucanians submitted to Rome without further resistance in 206 BC (Liv. XXVIII.11). Rome subsequently founded colonies at Buxentum in 194 BC (Vell. Pat. 1,15,3) and Thurii-Copia in 193 BC (Liv. XXXIV.53.1). The Lucanians later joined the Social War and, with the Samnites, resisted the longest (App. B Civ. 1,39). By Strabo’s time, most Lucanian poleis were deserted (VI.1.2; Meiggs, Lewis (2005), p. 827-828).

The Bruttians followed a slightly different path. During the Second Punic War, Petelia and Consentia were the principal loyal communities; Petelia and Consentia resisted Hannibal for almost a year in 216 BC (XXIII.20), and continued switching back and forth after their conquest (Liv. XXIII.30; XXV.1; XXIX.38; XXX.19). Bruttian support for Hannibal was driven largely by hopes of conquering and plundering the Italiote cities, alarming both the Greeks and Carthaginian commanders seeking allies. Rhegion barred its gates to the Carthaginians (Liv. XXIV.1), Lokroi submitted voluntarily for protection (XXIII.30), and the Crotoniates were resettled there to escape Bruttian pillaging (XXIV.3). Hanno’s army against Sempronius Gracchus included 17,000 infantry, mostly Lucanians and Bruttians, and Bruttium remained Hannibal’s refuge until his withdrawal to Africa (XXVII-XXVIII, XXX.20). Gold, silver and bronze issues inscribed “Brettion,” alongside Greek coins, date from this period (Meiggs, Lewis: Bruttii, Bruttium, in: DNP (2003), p. 794). After Hannibal’s return to Africa, Bruttians formed a large part of Hannibal’s elite reserve at Zama in 202 BC (XXX.33). Roman operations continued thereafter in fighting that Livy described as “closer to brigandage than regular warfare” (XXIX.6, XXX.40, even in 200 BC: XXXI.6). After extensive pillaging and punishment, colonies were established at Tempsa, Croton, Copia and Vibo (Gell. NA 10,3,19; Fest. 28,19; Liv. XXXIV.53; XXXV.95, XXXV.40). The Bruttian League was dissolved, the people’s political importance declined, and they did not join the Social War (91-89 BC). In the 2nd century BC, Rome extended the Via Annia or Popilia, linking Rome, Capua and Rhegion (CIL I2 638) and facilitating movement and better resource extraction in Bruttium (Capelletti: The Bruttii, in: Farney; Bradley: The Peoples of Italy (2018), p. 333-334).

The rosters of both factions are naturally the same with slight differences in terms of aesthetic looks. The inspiration is mainly taken from Paestan tomb paintings and Lucanian pottery. Lucanian tunics have more patterns and colours as they are the wealthier of the two:

Oscan Javelinmen: Javelin-fighting is very dominant in Southern Italy. The shields are so-called “parasol shields” as defined by Michael Burns in his thesis on Southern Italian Equipment (2006). Image 1 always shows the Lucanian version Image 2 the Bruttian version

Heavy Oscan Skirmishers are a heavier version of the javelinmen, and with superior armour can also enter close combat. The adoption of the round shield with spina is shown on Bruttian coinage.

Light Swordsmen are a shock unit, equipped with swords which are more prominent among Lucanian and Southern Italian depictions and graves than in Campania/Samnium.

Oscan Axemen, like the Samnite axemen, are equipped with armour-piercing axes and thureos shields.

Southern Oscan Spearmen, the lighest unit of the Southern Italic tribes. The pilos is probably adopted from the Greeks and more prominent in the south, compared to Campania and Samnium.

Oscan thureophoroi are a heavier version of the spearmen, protected by triple-disc cuirasses, a prominent piece of armour among Oscan peoples.

Oscan Hoplites have adopted the arms of Italiote Greeks as depicted on Paestan tomb paintings and Lucanian pottery. The star symbol is as popular here as it is in Campania and Samnium.

Oscan Light Cavalry is the most commonly depicted type of cavalry in Lucanian pottery, sometimes dubbed “ephebes” in historical literature.

Oscan Cavalry has adopted the Greek type of round cavalry shields, which appear quite commonly in Southern Italic depictions of cavalry and some Bruttian coinage.
